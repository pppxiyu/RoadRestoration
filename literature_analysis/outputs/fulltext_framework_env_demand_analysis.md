## Cross-paper observations

- **Environment component:** 14 of 18 have a clear decision→system-state module (Yes); 4 partial; 0 none. Even the pure-optimization papers usually embed one inside the objective/constraints (a network-loading or travel-time computation).
- **Traffic time/speed/volume:** 9 use it directly (Yes), 7 partially (e.g. fixed per-link times × a damage/repair delay coefficient, or a passenger-flow cost term without congestion), 2 not at all (topology/coverage only).
- **Dependence on OD-demand magnitude:** 13 depend on it, 4 do not, 1 n/a.
- **Demand magnitude static vs dynamic:** 5 hold the aggregate OD magnitude static over the recovery horizon; 12 make it time-varying; 1 has no OD demand.
- **What drives the dynamics (the key distinction for your study):** among the time-varying-demand papers, the change is **exogenous** (a preset time profile, independent of the network) in idx 1, 15, 763, 893, 1035, 1059, 1100, 1108, and **endogenous** (the demand responds to the restoration decisions / recovering network) in only idx 744, 878, 913, 2704. The endogenous cases are the closest to a demand that reacts to the repair policy.
- **Spatial resolution:** most resolve demand to the per-OD-pair (or per-station-pair) level; the debris-clearance / relief-logistics / interdependent-service papers (idx 99, 744, 878, 893, 913, 1035, 1108) instead use per-demand-node / per-zone demand.
- **Does the demand's dynamicness feed the objective?** the objective value genuinely changes with the demand trajectory in idx 1, 763, 913, 1100, 2704 (**Yes**); it does so for **one term only** in idx 15, 744, 878, 893, 1059, 1108 (**Partial**); and the time-variation does **not** reach the objective in idx 1035 (**No** — demand only gates feasibility/routing). The static-/no-demand papers are n/a. **The sharpest split for your study**, since your F1 is demand-weighted (a *Yes*).

*STRUCTURE AND INFRASTRUCTURE ENGINEERING, 2025.*  
*Confidence: high. Verifier: confirmed_all.*

*Connections.* Decision→state→objective loop. (A) The candidate repair sequence Xchr, together with a sampled realization of the uncertain recovery parameters (I0_v initial damage index, D_v delay duration, k_v recovery slope), drives the probabilistic bridge-recovery model: each bridge's damage index I_v(t) stays at I0_v through the delay phase then decreases linearly during the restoration phase (Fig. 2). (B) I_v(t) maps via Table 1 to residual link traffic capacity C_a(t) (100/70/50/20/0% for ND/SD/MD/ED/CD) and residual free-flow speed v0_a(t) (100/75/50/30/0%), hence free-flow time T0_a=L_a/v0_a (Eq. 2). (C) With the (possibly time-varying) OD demand TD_ij(t)=TD0_ij(1−Dr_t) (Eq. 7), the lower-level UE assignment (Beckmann objective Eq. 13, Frank-Wolfe) returns path flows f_p and link flows X_a(t) and BPR link times T_a(t) (Eq. 1). (D) These give ATT_t=Σ(T_a·X_a)/Σ TD_ij (Eq. 6), performance c_t=1/ATT (Eq. 5), functionality Q(t) normalized by best/worst performance (Eq. 8), and resilience R (Eq. 9). (E) Averaging R over y=1000 Latin-hypercube samples yields E(R) and the probabilistic-constraint estimate, which is the fitness the upper-level GA maximizes (BNRI-seeded initial population, fitness database, cut-off operator, selection/crossover/mutation). The upper level passes a sequence down; the lower level returns the traffic state/resilience up.

*How it realizes the objective.* Find the near-optimal repair SEQUENCE of the s damaged bridges that maximizes the expectation of network resilience E(R) (Eq. 10), subject to a resource constraint (number of bridges simultaneously under restoration cannot exceed Nmax repair teams, Eq. 12) and a probabilistic functionality constraint (P[Q(ta)≥h]≥β, Eq. 11). Resilience R (Eq. 9) is the horizon-normalized time-integral of network functionality Q(t) (Eq. 8), where Q is built from an average-travel-time performance metric c=1/ATT (Eqs. 5-6). The problem is a bi-level stochastic program: the upper level searches over repair orders; for each candidate order and each Latin-hypercube sample of the uncertain bridge-recovery parameters (I0_v, D_v, k_v), the lower-level UE traffic assignment evaluates the resulting traffic state and hence R; the Monte-Carlo mean over samples gives E(R) as the GA fitness. Thus a discrete repair-order decision is mapped to a probability-weighted resilience value and the order maximizing it (under crew/reliability constraints) is returned.

**Q2 — Environment component? Yes.**

There is an explicit decision→system-state evaluation block: the lower-level Wardrop user-equilibrium traffic-assignment model (Beckmann formulation, solved by Frank-Wolfe). It TAKES the decision — the repair sequence Xchr, which through the probabilistic recovery model determines which bridges are repaired and hence each affected link's residual capacity and free-flow speed at each time step — and RETURNS the resulting SYSTEM STATE: equilibrium path/link flows and congested link travel times, aggregated into total/average travel time, functionality Q(t), and resilience R. The paper states the lower layer "reflects the impact of the restoration strategy Xchr output from the upper layer on user traffic behavior" — precisely a decision→state map. It is not an RL environment but a network-loading/optimization evaluation module nested inside the objective/constraints, mapping an action (repair order + realized recovery) to a performance value.

- **Input (variable level):** Inputs to the UE-assignment environment at each time step: (1) the current network condition = which bridges are repaired vs. still damaged (from the candidate sequence Xchr + sampled recovery trajectory I_v(t)), which sets each affected link's residual traffic capacity C_a (100/70/50/20/0% of base for ND/SD/MD/ED/CD per Table 1) and residual free-flow speed v0_a (100/75/50/30/0%), hence free-flow travel time T0_a=L_a/v0_a; (2) the BPR volume-delay parameters α=0.15, β=4; (3) the OD demand vector q_w=TD_ij(t) between the 16 nodes (fixed pre-quake matrix in Case 1, or time-varying TD0_ij(1−Dr_t) in Case 2); (4) the path set / link-path incidence δ_ap for routing. Physically: given open/degraded links with their capacities and free-flow speeds, and a matrix of trips between the 16 nodes, find the user equilibrium.
- **Output (variable level):** Outputs: the user-equilibrium path flows f_p (satisfying Σf_p=q_w) and, aggregated, the link flows/volumes X_a(t) on every road segment, plus the corresponding congested link travel times T_a(t) from the BPR function (Eq. 1). These combine into total system travel time TTT=Σ T_a·X_a and the headline average travel time ATT_t=TTT/(total OD demand) (Eq. 6), then performance c_t=1/ATT (Eq. 5), functionality Q(t)∈[0,1] (Eq. 8), and resilience R∈[0,1] (Eq. 9). Concrete reported values: pre-quake TTT=1,313,207.6321 min, ATT=9.7275 min; immediately post-quake ATT=17.8253 min (TTT=2,406,414.3216 min). So the environment returns link volumes, link travel times, system travel time, per-trip average travel time, and the derived functionality/resilience.

**Q3 — Traffic metrics & OD demand.** Uses traffic time/speed/volume: **Yes**.

- *Traffic metrics used:* Core metric is travel TIME weighted by traffic VOLUME/FLOW: ATT=Σ_a(link travel time T_a × link flow X_a)/(total OD demand) (Eq. 6). Link travel time T_a comes from the BPR volume-delay function (Eq. 1), which depends on the flow/capacity ratio X_a/C_a and the free-flow time; SPEED enters via free-flow time T0_a=L_a/v0_a (Eq. 2) and via the residual-free-flow-speed reduction for damaged links (Table 1). The paper explicitly contrasts ATT against total travel time (TTT, Eq. 21), total travel distance (TTD, Eq. 22), a combined time+distance cost (Eq. 23), and a weighted-average-travel-speed metric (WATS), arguing ATT — a per-user time metric — is preferable because TTT/TTD spuriously improve when demand drops (Sec 4.4). So time, speed, and volume are all used; the functionality/resilience is time-based.
- *Depends on OD-demand magnitude:* **Yes**
- *How demand magnitude is determined:* Demand magnitude is a FIXED GIVEN pre-earthquake OD matrix (Table 4), a full 16×16 node-to-node matrix in pcu/h (values ~250–950), used directly as the baseline — it is not estimated from data or a gravity model in this application. Post-earthquake demand is this given matrix scaled uniformly by a demand reduction rate: TD_ij(t)=TD0_ij(1−Dr_t) (Eq. 7). Dr_t follows the Shinozuka et al. (2008) linear model (Eq. 3): it jumps to an initial value Dr0 (=40%, from Liu et al. 2021) right after the quake, then decreases linearly to zero over the recovery period Tdr=365/(10−MMI) days (Eq. 4), with MMI=8 network-wide → Tdr≈182.5 days≈6 months. The paper notes that for large networks with spatially varying MMI a reduction model plus a gravity distribution model could produce a variable OD matrix, but that is explicitly NOT done here ("a same MMI is applied to the entire transportation network in this paper").
- *Magnitude static or dynamic:* **Dynamic**
- *Dynamicness trigger (self vs decision-making):* EXOGENOUS. The paper studies two cases: Case 1 holds post-quake demand fixed at the pre-quake level (STATIC magnitude), and Case 2 (the framework's headline contribution) uses a time-varying demand. In Case 2 the demand magnitude follows a preset time profile driven only by earthquake intensity (MMI) and elapsed time — it drops to Dr0=40% at t0 and recovers linearly to full over Tdr≈6 months, regardless of which bridges are repaired. It is NOT a response to the restoration decisions or the evolving network state; demand recovery and link-capacity recovery run on independent clocks. Evidence of exogeneity: "network functionality of the NOS fluctuates during the traffic demand recovery period (first 6 months)" — an exogenous demand effect, not endogenous feedback. Day-to-day traffic flow re-equilibrates via UE, but the OD MAGNITUDE trajectory is preset by the MMI/time model, not by the schedule.
- *Spatial/temporal resolution:* Spatial: per-OD-pair — a full 16-node × 16-node origin-destination matrix TD_ij (Table 4), assigned to links via UE routing, giving per-link flows X_a as output. Temporal: a single scalar reduction factor Dr_t is applied uniformly to every OD pair (single network-wide MMI=8), evolving over the recovery horizon; analysis is discretized into unit time steps Ut over a 24-month horizon (th), with demand recovery completing at ~6 months and a cut-off operator (Eq. 20) truncating time steps after all repairs finish.
- *Other demand notes:* Rerouting is endogenous through Wardrop user equilibrium (Beckmann formulation, Frank-Wolfe): travelers redistribute over paths as links degrade/recover. The aggregate demand drop is a lumped proxy for behavioral responses the paper names — trip cancellation and switching to public transport — with no explicit mode-split or elasticity model; those effects are folded into Dr0. No latent/induced demand, no agent-based trips. Demand reduction is uniform across all OD pairs (no spatial differentiation of who cancels trips), a stated simplification. The framework is single-hazard (no aftershocks/tsunami/landslide — an acknowledged limitation), assumes only bridges are damageable, uses uniform distributions for the three uncertain recovery parameters, and (base case) a constant number of repair crews (Nmax=1 in the example; the paper notes dynamic crew numbers are outside its scope). Section 4.5 extends to bi-objective resilience-vs-restoration-cost with a Pareto front.

**Q4 — Does the demand's dynamicness affect the objective function? Yes.**

The single optimized objective is Max E(R) (Eq. 10), where network resilience R is the horizon-normalized time-integral of functionality Q(t) (Eq. 9). Q(t) is a normalization of the performance metric c_t (Eq. 8), and c_t = 1/ATT_t (Eq. 5). The average-travel-time ATT_t (Eq. 6) is demand-weighted: its denominator is the sum of the per-period OD demand TD_ij^t, and TD_ij^t = TD_ij^0*(1 - Dr_t) (Eq. 7) where the demand-reduction rate Dr_t decays linearly over the recovery period (Eq. 3). Thus the time-varying demand magnitude enters the objective directly: as demand recovers over months, ATT_t (and therefore c_t, Q(t), and the integral R) changes, so the objective value depends on the demand trajectory. The paper demonstrates this empirically — the fixed-demand case gives E(R)=0.8550 while the dynamic-demand case gives E(R)=0.8918 with a DIFFERENT near-optimal restoration sequence, and it concludes that ignoring the post-quake demand decline underestimates optimal resilience. Section 4.4 further shows ATT was chosen precisely BECAUSE it correctly folds dynamic demand into the objective (unlike TTT/TTD, which mis-scale when demand drops). Note: the §4.5 multi-objective extension adds a second objective C = discounted total restoration cost (Eq. 24), which is demand-independent; but the paper's core/primary objective (network resilience) is fully scaled by the dynamic demand, so dynamicness does affect the objective.

*Connections.* Data flow: the GA (using recovery/cost functions and the STAM-1 significance indices for its cheap fitness) produces 10 candidate schedules -> each schedule defines quarter-by-quarter bridge damage ratios d^t_i via the recovery functions -> these reduce link capacities in the disrupted network -> STAM-2 runs the snapshot ICFM II on that reduced-capacity network (producing O-D flows, link flows, and a transportation-cost objective Z) and computes the zonal disruption ratio from link/O-D flows -> the disruption ratio feeds the snapshot FDLM to get final-demand loss -> NEL = transportation-cost change + final-demand loss, summed over quarters (Table 7). Temporal recursion (Figure 1): state at time t (changed O-D flows, link flows, delta y) conveys to t+1, the network progressively recovering per the chosen strategy until damage reaches zero. Full STAM-2 (>3 min/strategy) is run only on the GA's 10 finalists; the winner is the shortest-duration, lowest-NEL strategy (Strategy 10: 8 quarters, $5.45B).

*How it realizes the objective.* Find the best post-earthquake bridge-reconstruction schedule that (a) minimizes total reconstruction duration (weighted higher, since complete reconstruction = complete economic restoration) and (b) minimizes network economic loss (transportation-cost increase + final-demand loss), subject to a per-quarter budget constraint (Eq 2) and infrastructure recovery-ratio dynamics (Eqs 3-5). Realized by GA search over schedules ranked by a duration-plus-economic-significance fitness (Eq 8), then exact economic evaluation of the top-10 via STAM-2; the shortest-duration, smallest-NEL strategy wins (Strategy 10: 8 quarters = 2 years, $5.45B loss). The paper explicitly frames itself as a concept-proving framework (validation/calibration deferred).

**Q2 — Environment component? Yes.**

STAM-2 is an explicit decision->state->performance environment, and it embeds the network-loading step that optimization papers often hide. It takes a reconstruction decision (the repair schedule, which specifies how each bridge's damage ratio, hence link capacity, recovers each quarter) and returns the resulting system state and performance. The snapshot ICFM II is a commodity-flow assignment/network-loading module: it loads MRIO-derived demand onto the capacity-reduced network, produces O-D and link flows, and yields an objective value Z read as transportation cost, with increased link cost driving rerouting. The zonal-disruption + FDLM block maps the same disrupted state to a final-demand loss. Together they convert a decision into per-quarter performance values (transportation cost, final-demand loss, NEL) — the decision->state->performance mapping asked for. **Q4 — Does the demand's dynamicness affect the objective function? Partial.**

The paper optimizes a two-criteria objective: (a) shortest total reconstruction DURATION and (b) minimum NETWORK ECONOMIC LOSS = ΔTC (change in ICFM commodity-flow transportation cost) + ΔFD (final demand loss). Hence one objective term (makespan/duration) is unaffected by demand dynamicness while the other (economic loss, dominated by final-demand loss and flow cost) is driven by it → partial.

*Connections.* Closed RL loop over daily steps. Each step every agent reads its state vector s_t (Eq.9) and, using the DQN with decreasing epsilon-greedy plus messages m_t^k from other agents, selects one disrupted road to repair. The chosen actions (crew workloads W_k) enter the human-mobility estimation environment: cumulative workload on each road updates its S-curve progress P_m,t (Eq.4), raising the number of open lanes and thus road capacity (initially zero). Recovered capacity (i) endogenously revives suppressed per-OD demand q(rs),t via Assumption 2 (demand governed by the minimum cumulative progress of the disrupted roads that OD passes) and (ii) feeds the stochastic traffic assignment, which splits each OD's demand across feasible paths in proportion to each path's minimum available capacity (Eq.5, Assumption 3) and sums path flows into link flows V_a,t (Eq.6). Link flows are compared with normal-day flows to compute per-road TR_K,t and overall TR_O,t recovery rates (Eq.7). These recovery rates, together with operation progress, agent travel time, cooperation effects CPA and traffic weight alpha_rt, become the next state and the reward (basic R = change in mobility recovery rate scaled by alpha_rt; extra F = travel-time cost + cooperation bonus W_t). If overall recovery exceeds 75% the episode ends and the policy is updated from accumulated experience; otherwise the loop repeats to the 35-step limit.

*How it realizes the objective.* The objective — recover disrupted human mobility as rapidly as possible after multi-locational flooding — is realized because the basic reward IS the change in a traffic-volume-based human-mobility recovery rate (per-road change scaled by the road's traffic weight alpha_rt), so agents learn to prioritize roads whose reopening restores the most real travel; they end up selecting high-betweenness-centrality, high-traffic urban roads first (Fig.11/Fig.12). The environment makes this data-driven and dynamic: GPS-derived OD demand plus STA translate each repair into realistic link flows rather than static topological measures, so a repair's value reflects actual road usage. Cooperation shaping and the communication protocol let multiple crews concurrently restore inter-connected roads serving shared OD trips, further speeding aggregate recovery. The learned multi-agent policy reliably reaches the 75% mobility target, and its final human-mobility recovery rate is about 20% better than the lowest rate obtained by random crew scheduling, yielding a generalizable post-disaster road-management plan (stated combinatorial space ~2.32e52, solved in ~3 h for 45 roads).

**Q2 — Environment component? Yes.**

There is an explicit RL environment: the "human mobility estimation model" (Section 3.2) takes the agents' repair actions and returns the resulting network/traffic state and a performance metric. Section 3 states the loop directly: after an action, "the attribute of each damage road (e.g., travel time and, basic capacity) and travel demand of each origin-destination (O-D) pair are recovered and updated. Thereafter, the traffic flow on each link is estimated and the human mobility recovery rate is calculated." Concretely it updates road capacity from cumulative repair work via an S-curve (Eq.4), regenerates per-OD demand (Assumption 2), runs a stochastic traffic assignment to compute path and link flows (Eqs.5-6), and outputs a traffic-volume recovery rate (Eq.7) that feeds back into the RL state and basic reward. This is a genuine decision-to-performance (network-loading) mapping, not a static objective.

- **Input (variable level):** Variable-level inputs: (1) the repair action(s) each day, expressed as crew workload W_k applied to specific damaged roads; (2) the cumulative workload sum_{k=0..t} W_k on each road m relative to its total workload S_m, where S_m = road length x lane width x a damage-risk weight (Table 3: inundation depth / sediment-hazard level, weights 1-3), yielding S-curve progress P_m,t (Eq.4, lambda=0.8, calibrated so ~14 days of max operation completes a road); (3) the recovered road capacity, defined as the maximum traffic on the currently open lanes (starts at zero, grows with progress); (4) the base per-OD travel demand for ~1000-1270 OD pairs (from the GPS OD matrix scaled by per-1km-grid commuter/resident counts), itself modulated by road progress via Assumption 2; (5) network topology G=(N,A) from the Japan Digital Road Map, the path set K_rs per OD, and each path's minimum link capacity C_k,t (Assumption 3).
- **Output (variable level):** Variable-level outputs: (1) per-road cumulative progress rate P_m,t (fraction of capacity/lanes restored); (2) path usage share U_k,t = C_k,t / sum_k C_k,t and path flow F_k,t = q(rs),t * U_k,t (Eq.5) — how an OD's trips split across routes by each route's minimum available capacity; (3) link flow / traffic volume V_a,t = sum over ODs and paths of F_k,t*delta (Eq.6) — estimated vehicles on every link at step t; (4) per-damaged-road recovery rate TR_K,t = (sum of link flows on road K at t) / (sum of normal-day link flows on K) and the overall network recovery rate TR_O,t (Eq.7). TR, plus travel time and cooperation terms, is fed back into the RL state and basic reward. The network metric is traffic VOLUME, not congested travel time: there is no BPR / volume-delay function; assignment allocates flow purely by relative link capacity, and travel time appears only as the crew's operation-cost distance.

**Q3 — Traffic metrics & OD demand.** Uses traffic time/speed/volume: **Yes**.

- *Traffic metrics used:* The core performance metric is traffic VOLUME / flow: link flows V_a,t (Eq.6) aggregated into a recovery rate TR = current-vs-normal-day link-flow ratio (Eq.7), used as both a state input and the basic reward. The authors state explicitly: "We select the traffic volume as the recovery evaluation index." Travel TIME is used only in a limited, non-congestion sense — as the crew's operation cost (agent's travel from a fixed start point to the worksite, entered as a negative reward). Post-hoc betweenness centrality (shortest-path counts) is used only to interpret which roads agents chose, not as a performance metric. There is no volume-delay/BPR travel-time function and no travel-time or speed performance metric on the network; the assignment allocates flow by relative link capacity, not by congested travel time. Speed is not used.
- *Depends on OD-demand magnitude:* **Yes**
- *How demand magnitude is determined:* Demand comes from an empirical OD matrix built from Agoop mobile-phone GPS data (0.26% sample rate, 3817 users, Hiroshima Prefecture): ~15,000 ODs detected, 1,270 pass through flood-damaged roads, ~1,000 used to estimate mobility. The per-OD demand magnitude is estimated using MLIT local geographic information — the number of commuters and residents in each 1 km grid ("We used the number of commuters and residents of each 1 km grid to estimate the hourly travel demand of the target O-Ds"). So base demand is data-derived (empirical GPS OD flows scaled by commuter/resident counts), not a gravity model or uniform assumption. Normal-day link flows F_a,n (the recovery-rate denominator) are the assignment of this full pre-disaster demand.
- *Magnitude static or dynamic:* **Dynamic**
- *Dynamicness trigger (self vs decision-making):* Endogenous. Per Assumption 2, each OD's travel demand q(rs),t is governed by the possibility of alternative routes — specifically the minimum cumulative progress among the disrupted roads that OD passes on normal days. ODs are isolated (demand suppressed) when their roads are impassable, and "the traffic in the network is increased to the pre-disaster level as the damaged roads are restored." Thus the demand MAGNITUDE (not merely the flow split) recovers as a direct response to the agents' restoration decisions and the evolving network state, not from a preset exogenous time profile. This is the time-varying-magnitude case, not the static-magnitude trap. (The paper gives Assumption 2 qualitatively and does not print the exact demand-recovery equation, so the precise functional form is unclear, but the driver is clearly endogenous.)
- *Spatial/temporal resolution:* Spatial: per-OD-pair (~1,000-1,270 pairs), with base demand estimated at 1 km-grid zone level (commuters/residents per 1 km grid; OD stay-points detected on a 1 km grid). Flows are resolved per link and per path. Temporal: daily time steps (1 step = 1 day; episodes up to 35 steps), with base demand estimated at an hourly level ("hourly travel demand of the target O-Ds"); the demand magnitude is updated each daily step as road progress changes.
- *Other demand notes:* Mode is fixed to private vehicles on roads only ("we assumed that the mode of transportation was only the vehicles on the road"); no mode split or transit. Rerouting IS modeled: within the STA, demand is re-split across feasible paths each step in proportion to each path's minimum available capacity (Eq.5), so trips reroute as capacities recover. Demand is elastic/latent in that suppressed trips reappear as roads reopen (Assumption 2), but there is no gravity, induced-demand, or agent-level trip simulation — it is aggregate per-OD demand. Only weekday daytime OD locations are used (stated limitation; weekend/evacuation-location changes are future work). GPS raw records use a 100 m grid ID, but stay-point detection and OD demand use a 1 km grid.

**Q4 — Does the demand's dynamicness affect the objective function? Yes.**

The RL objective is "maximizing the sum of the discounted future rewards," where the basic reward is defined as the change in the human mobility recovery rate (scaled per road by the traffic weight alpha_rt), plus an agent-travel-time term and a cooperation term. The human mobility recovery rate (Eq. 7) is TR = sum_a F_{a,t} / sum_a F_{a,n}, i.e., current estimated traffic flow over normal-day traffic flow. Current flow F_{a,t} is constructed from the per-step OD travel demand q(rs),t via F_k,t = q(rs),t * U_k,t (Eq. 5) and V_{a,t} = sum F_k,t (Eq. 6). That demand is explicitly time-varying: Assumption 2 states the travel demand of each OD grows with the cumulative progress of the disrupted roads it passes, rising back toward the pre-disaster level as roads are restored. Therefore the dynamic (recovering) demand directly scales the traffic-flow numerator of the recovery rate at every step, and the reward is the step-to-step change of that rate. The demand's time-variation IS the driver of the objective's value; a static demand would produce a flat recovery-rate trajectory and no reward signal. Hence the dynamicness propagates fully into the objective (not merely into constraints/state). This is the subtle served-demand/traffic-volume-evaluated-with-per-period-demand case = yes.

*Connections.* The damage model turns inspected bridge damage into reduced arc capacities C_i and reduced free-flow speeds/times T0_i (Table 2) and flags closed arcs. These feed the traffic module, which distributes the fixed OD demand (Gravity Model) and assigns it by capacity-limited incremental User-Equilibrium, producing link volumes V_i and, through the BPR function, link travel times T_i and speeds S_i (Eq 4). The WATS module aggregates S_i with length/capacity weights w_i into WATS(t), normalized to functionality Q(t) (Eqs 1-3). A candidate schedule X sets each bridge's start time; the time-varying damage index I_i^t (Eq 13) causes an arc to regain its capacity/speed only when its bridge is fully repaired (step-function recovery, a bridge held at initial damage until completion), so re-running the traffic module at each bridge-completion time traces the whole Q(t) curve. From that curve the three indicators RRF, RRS, PRT (and RL) are computed. NSGA-II (Model 1) or GA (Model 2) mutates/recombines schedules X, re-evaluates Q(t) and the indicators, and converges to the Pareto set / single optimum, all under N_rs^t <= NTmax=3.

*How it realizes the objective.* The stated goal is to find general optimal restoration schedules that reduce indirect loss (assumed proportional to resilience loss) without pre-committing to any single decision-maker preference. The framework realizes this by (a) using WATS so degradation is judged correctly even when post-disaster demand changes, (b) decomposing the recovery curve into three independent normalized indicators (RRF fixed by damage; RRS = crew-distribution/Combination; PRT = repair-sequence/Permutation) so schedules differing only in assignment or sequence are distinguishable, and (c) running NSGA-II to return a small Pareto set of non-dominated schedules (29 here) instead of one answer, letting decision-makers apply their own weighting/experience afterward, which reduces problem dimensionality and computation. Model 2 (single-objective RL minimization via GA) is a comparison model whose unique optimum lies on the same Pareto front.

PSO iterates (with random-search re-diversification) until it converges on the y that minimizes the generalized cost. The road and terminal restorations are coupled because terminal throughput requires both operational internal roads and functioning unloading equipment.

*Connections.* The data module builds the graph and the component→arc enabling sets AC/EC. The recovery part outputs β_ct (component installed / arc unlocked). β_ct feeds the relocation part through constraint (3.6) — a responder may traverse a facility–facility arc (j,j') only if some component in EC(j,j') is operational — and feeds the coverage part through constraint (3.10) — demand i may be covered via disrupted arc (i,j)∈A' only if some component in AC(i,j) is operational. The relocation flow y determines which facilities are 'open' each period; (3.9) ties δ_ijt to a responder actually being at facility j; (3.8) sums coverage into levels z_iℓt, which objective (3.1) weights by w_it and θ_ℓ. Decision flow: crew schedule → arc availability → (permits responder relocation via 3.6) and (permits demand coverage via 3.10) → coverage levels z → objective Z. Constraints (3.6) and (3.10) are the explicit couplings (the paper names them as the two linking constraint sets, and (3.10) is the one dualized in the Lagrangian heuristic).

*How it realizes the objective.* The assembled MIP maximizes cumulative multiple (primary + backup) coverage of population-weighted emergency demand over a finite horizon by jointly choosing (a) the K-crew component-repair schedule and (b) the relocations of P emergency responders, subject to the constraint that responders can only move over — and demand can only be covered through — arcs that are initially available or have been unlocked by completed repairs, plus relocation-distance restrictions (≤2 mi). Non-decreasing weights θ_ℓ reward backup coverage to capture responder unavailability under high post-disaster demand. Solving it (exactly via GUROBI or via the Lagrangian/LP-rounding heuristics) yields a prioritized restoration plan plus a responder-movement plan; per-period Z_t and coverage ratios Z_t/G* diagnose how fast coverage recovers as repairs proceed. The paper notes the model reduces to a static coverage problem if y is replaced by a fixed y_j, and can be trivially recast as a P-median variant minimizing weighted distance to the L closest open facilities.

**Q2 — Environment component? Partial.**

There is no simulator, no RL environment, and — crucially — no traffic-assignment / network-loading / travel-time step. However, the coverage constraint block (3.8)–(3.12) together with objective (3.1) does function as an evaluation environment in the optimization sense: given an action — a repair schedule (β_ct, i.e. which arcs are made passable) and a responder placement (flow y, i.e. which facilities are open) — it returns a resulting system state (per-demand coverage indicators δ_ijt and z_iℓt) and a performance value (Z, Z_t). It is only 'partial' because this mapping is purely combinatorial/topological: arc availability is set directly by the binary repair decision (a component is installed or not), and coverage is a binary distance-threshold test (is the demand node within the pre-computed ≤0.7-mi coverage set of an open facility reachable through an available arc). No congestion, travel time, flow volume, speed, or total-system-travel-time is ever computed, so it evaluates a coverage state, not a physical traffic state.

- **Input (variable level):** Action / decision inputs: which components are operational by time t (β_ct → equivalently which disrupted arcs in A' and E' have become passable) and where each of the P responders is located each period (flow variables y_{j,j',t}, defining the set of open facilities). Fixed parameters: population-based demand weight w_it at each demand node; pre-computed static eligibility sets N_i^A (facilities covering i via an already-available arc within 0.7 mi), AC(i,j) (components whose installation lets facility j cover demand i within 0.7 mi), EC(j,j') (components whose installation lets responders move j↔j' within 2 mi); number of coverage levels L; marginal weights θ_ℓ. In physical terms: open/closed status of each road component, responder-to-facility assignments, a fixed per-node demand quantity, and walkable-distance (0.7 mi) / relocation-distance (2 mi) proximity relationships.
- **Output (variable level):** Binary coverage indicators δ_ijt (demand node i covered by facility j at time t through a repaired arc) and z_iℓt (demand node i covered by at least ℓ facilities at t, i.e. primary vs backup coverage), plus the scalar objective Z = Σ_t Σ_ℓ Σ_i w_it θ_ℓ z_iℓt with per-period decomposition Z_t. Reported physical outputs are the counts of demand nodes covered by a primary facility and by a backup facility each period (e.g. K=5: 129 primary, 41 backup, Z=1804.01 in one instance), the population-weighted coverage value, and coverage ratios Z_t/G* relative to a fully-functional network. No link travel time, link flow/volume, average speed, or total-system-travel-time is produced — the output is a coverage / served-demand state, not a traffic state.

**Q3 — Traffic metrics & OD demand.** Uses traffic time/speed/volume: **No**.

- *Traffic metrics used:* No traffic time, speed, or volume is computed. Service accessibility is modeled purely as static spatial proximity plus arc availability: a facility covers a demand node if their real road distance (computed with GeoPy) is ≤0.7 miles — a 'walkable' three-avenue-block threshold — AND the connecting arc is available (initially, or after repair); responders may relocate only between facilities ≤2 miles apart. Travel time enters only as an a priori fixed threshold used to define which arcs qualify as coverage/relocation arcs ('If the arcs for relocating emergency responders are defined as those whose travel times are within a certain time threshold parameter, the relocation times between any pair of facility locations are approximately the same'), never as a quantity computed from flow or congestion. The performance metric is cumulative multiple (primary + backup) coverage of population-weighted demand — a served-demand / coverage count, not a flow- or delay-based traffic metric.
- *Depends on OD-demand magnitude:* **n/a**
- *How demand magnitude is determined:* There is no OD (origin–destination) demand matrix. Demand is a per-node scalar w_it estimated from data: for each demand node (census-tract center) it is the number of Hurricane-Sandy '311' damaged-tree service complaints associated with the node, multiplied by the proportional population of the associated census tract, to approximate the number of possible emergency-service requests. It is a demand-at-node (coverage) quantity, not a flow between origins and destinations; there is no gravity model, no uniform-demand assumption, and no traffic assignment.
- *Magnitude static or dynamic:* **Static**
- *Dynamicness trigger (self vs decision-making):* na — the paper explicitly assumes the demand weight w_it is fixed over the horizon for each node ('Note that we assume the weight w_it is fixed over the time horizon T for each demand node i∈D'). What changes over time is not demand magnitude but the network's repair state (which arcs are available) and the responder locations; coverage improves as components are installed, but the underlying demand quantity is neither exogenously time-profiled nor endogenously responsive.
- *Spatial/temporal resolution:* Spatial resolution: per demand node = per census tract, with |D| = 276 demand nodes in the Bronx Borough case study and |J| = 38 facility locations (32 fire/rescue stations + 6 hurricane evacuation centers). Demand is a single scalar per node (not per OD-pair, not per link). Temporal resolution: discrete periods t = 1,…,T over a one-day horizon, with T ∈ {8, 12, 18}, but w_it is held constant across all periods.
- *Other demand notes:* No mode split, no elasticity, no rerouting/assignment of trips, no agent-based trips, no latent/induced demand. Demand at a node is unmet unless a covering facility with a responder is reachable within the distance threshold; 'backup coverage' (multiple coverage weighted by θ_ℓ, e.g. θ_1=0.7, θ_2=0.3, and an L=3 variant with 0.5/0.3/0.2) models responder unavailability under high post-disaster request volume. Accessibility is topological/distance-based (arc availability + ≤0.7-mi coverage, ≤2-mi relocation), not congestion-based. Sensitivity instances vary J0, complaint threshold (3 vs 5), relocation distance (1 vs 2 mi), coverage distance (0.7 vs 1.0 mi), L (2 vs 3), and P (8 vs 10). A P-median variant minimizing weighted distance to the L closest open facilities is noted as a trivial extension.

**Q4 — Does the demand's dynamicness affect the objective function? n/a (static demand).**

The optimized objective (3.1) is max Z = Σ_{t=1..T} Σ_{ℓ=1..L} Σ_{i∈D} w_it θ_ℓ z_iℓt — maximizing cumulative multiple coverage of emergency-service demand over the horizon, where the demand weight w_it directly scales each per-period coverage term z_iℓt (with θ_ℓ the marginal coverage benefit at level ℓ). So the objective value IS demand-weighted. However, the demand magnitude itself is NOT time-varying: the paper explicitly assumes w_it is fixed over the time horizon T for every demand node (w_it = w_i for all t). What actually changes over time (Z_t rises across periods) is the coverage z_iℓt, driven by arc restoration and responder relocation, not the demand level. Because demand is static, there is no dynamicness of demand to propagate into the objective — hence n/a. The time index on w_it is nominal only.

*Connections.* The framework is a closed loop that mirrors the authors' bilevel program (Fig. 2). At each MDP stage k, the agent (outer level) selects a decision d_k = a subset of damaged links L'_k to restore. The crew-allocation submodel (Model 11-15) converts that subset into an optimal crew assignment {w*} and a min-makespan completion time ϑ*_{L'_k}, which also serves as the feasibility filter (a decision is feasible iff the model has an optimal solution under Λ_k). The chosen links are reintroduced into the topology (L' is reduced), and the day-to-day traffic-assignment environment (inner level) simulates traveler rerouting from the current flow pattern X toward target flows Y a fraction α per day, computing BPR link travel times and total system travel cost across the horizon. The reward calculator differences total travel cost of the network WITHOUT vs. WITH the newly restored links over [ϑ''+1, T] to produce r_k (Eqs 9-10). That reward flows back to the agent's Q-learning update (Eq. 16), which revises value estimates and, over many iterations with resampled stochastic repair times, converges to π* = argmax Σ γ^k r_k (Eq. 17). Thus decisions flow outer→inner (which links open, and their crew-set completion time) and performance flows inner→outer (travel-time reduction) — exactly the information exchange the authors state is required at each bilevel iteration.

*How it realizes the objective.* Maximize post-disaster traffic acceleration — the reduction in total network travel time/cost during the response window [0,T] (e.g., T=60 days) — under scarce crews and uncertain repair times. The framework realizes this by: (a) making the objective a direct traffic quantity (horizon-summed reduction in Σ link travel-time × flow) rather than a surrogate like connectivity/accessibility; (b) evaluating every candidate schedule through a day-to-day, non-equilibrium traffic simulation that captures transient fluctuations and traveler inertia after each link reopens, so the agent sees the true short-term traffic impact rather than an end-state equilibrium; (c) sequencing decisions through a multi-stage MDP so schedules adapt to the evolving network and stage-varying crew availability; and (d) sampling stochastic repair times inside the RL iterations so the learned policy is robust on average. Q-learning searches the schedule/crew-allocation space efficiently to find the restoration ordering that yields the largest cumulative travel-time reduction. A notable finding is that faster/more-crew restoration does NOT always increase acceleration, because compressing intervals between successive network deformations amplifies transitional fluctuations.

There is an explicit RL learning environment: the 'learning environment of TARM,' an optimization-based day-to-day traffic-assignment module (He et al. [63], Model 3-8) plus the traffic-acceleration reward calculator (Eqs 9-10). It receives an action from the agent (restore a specific subset of links L'', which reopens those links at a computed stochastic completion time ϑ'') and returns the resulting system state and performance: evolving per-link flows, BPR link travel times, total network travel cost, and the derived travel-time-reduction reward. This is precisely a decision→system-state→performance mapping — the inner level of the paper's own bilevel formulation ('the impact of these decisions on the traffic cost/time of the road network is evaluated in the RL's learning environment which corresponds to the inner level of bilevel programming'). Both an explicit RL environment interface and an optimization evaluation block are present, so the answer is unambiguously yes.

- **Input (variable level):** Which links are open vs. closed: the available-link set L−L', updated each time the agent's chosen subset L'' is reopened (a link is fully unavailable until its repair completes; no partial reopening). Per-link BPR supply attributes: free-flow travel time c0, nominal capacity B_(n,m), and current link flow x_(n,m). The post-disaster OD demand matrix d'_{i,j} over post-disaster OD pairs M' (origins O', destinations E'). The current network flow pattern X = [x^{ij}_{nm}] used as the day-to-day starting point. - **Output (variable level):** Per-OD/per-link target flows Y = [y^{ij}_{nm}] from the inner LP (Model 3-8), which minimizes a weighted sum of total travel cost Σ c_(n,m)(x)·y_(n,m) (weight λ) and travelers' route-change inertia Σ|x^{ij}_{nm} − y^{ij}_{nm}| (weight λ') subject to OD flow-conservation constraints (4-6). Finally the reward r(L'') = Σ_{t=ϑ''+1}^{T} [ (total travel cost with links still closed, flows x') − (total travel cost with links reopened, flows x) ] (Eqs 9-10) — the horizon-integrated reduction in total network travel time from the restoration action. In physical terms: link volumes, link travel times, total system travel time, and how much total travel time the restoration saved.

**Q3 — Traffic metrics & OD demand.** Uses traffic time/speed/volume: **Yes**.

Benchmarks use non-traffic betweenness centrality (flow-unaware) and flow-weighted centrality (Π' = Σ β·d') as comparison criteria; TARM outperforms both (up to 68.4% vs. flow-unaware, up to 57% vs. flow-aware).

- *How demand magnitude is determined:* The post-disaster OD demand d'_{i,j} is a given/estimated exogenous matrix, not solved endogenously. In the case study, OD pairs and travel demands are adjusted according to the tornado's movement path, with node-level demands set proportional to the local population of the corresponding urban districts (a population-proportional heuristic, not a fitted gravity model; §5.1, p.9). New post-disaster OD pairs (e.g., casualty/relief flows) can emerge and some pre-disaster demands drop, but magnitudes are exogenously specified inputs. Deployment guidance notes demand can be sourced from incident reports, field assessments, and probe/detector traffic sensing.

- *Dynamicness trigger (self vs decision-making):* Demand magnitude is assumed constant within the short-term restoration horizon: 'For modeling purposes, OD demands are assumed constant within the short-term restoration process' (§3 assumptions, p.5). What evolves day-to-day is the traffic FLOW/route assignment (travelers rerouting a fraction α per day as links reopen), NOT the total OD demand — this is the classic 'flow evolves but magnitude is static' case. The framework permits demand to be updated between MDP stages ('OD demands can be updated between stages — added, removed, or adjusted — if such data is available'), but that would be an exogenous, data-driven update supplied by the modeler, not an endogenous response of demand magnitude to the restoration decisions or recovering network. Temporal resolution: a single demand magnitude per OD pair held constant over the [0,T] horizon (T = 60 days), while the assignment/route-choice update operates on a daily (day-to-day) timestep.
- *Other demand notes:* Route choice/rerouting is central: the day-to-day model (He et al. [63]) blends travel-time minimization with behavioral inertia, tuned by λ/λ' (cost vs. inertia weight, tested at 0.1/0.67/1/1.5/9) and α (daily shift ratio / info-spread speed, tested at 0.1/0.2/0.4/0.6). Travelers adapt individually without central coordination, so faster info spread (higher α) can amplify transient fluctuations rather than help — a key insight; the authors note ATIS-coordinated routing would instead speed convergence (future work). Assignment is deterministic, single traveler class (uniform adaptation), with a noted extension to heterogeneous classes (commuters, evacuees, responders) by indexing demand/flows/behavioral params by class. No demand elasticity, no mode split, no explicit induced/latent demand, and not agent-based (it is a flow/link-based day-to-day dynamical system, Ż = α(Y−X)). Post-disaster demand can shift vs. pre-disaster (some OD pairs shrink, new emergency OD pairs appear) but only as exogenous inputs. Links are binary available/unavailable (no partial reopening or capacity degradation modeled).

**Q4 — Does the demand's dynamicness affect the objective function? n/a (static demand).**

*Connections.* A closed online loop per period t. (1) The Bayesian predictor (4.1.1) turns past regional observations into lambda and alpha and, with psi/mu, populates the belief state bS_t (4.1.2). **Q4 — Does the demand's dynamicness affect the objective function? Partial.**

The objective is therefore a weighted sum of THREE kinds of terms with different demand-dependence. (1) The served-demand benefit terms b_i·k_i and b_i·(rd_i1 − rd_it) DO depend on the time-varying demand: k_i is the amount of demand served this period (bounded by the remaining demand, constraint 14.8: k_i ≤ rd_i), and rd_it is the remaining unsatisfied demand which evolves period to period (transition Eq. 21). As remaining demand is drawn down and served over the horizon, this cumulative served-demand contribution grows and directly scales the objective height each period — exactly the throughput/served-demand-weighted case where dynamicness propagates into the objective value. (2) The travel-cost term Σ ψ_i t_ij X_ij (equipment request rate × travel time) and (3) the relocation-cost term σ Σ r_ij R_ij are functions of equipment positioning/movement, NOT of the demand magnitude, so their contribution is independent of demand dynamicness. Because one objective component is driven by the evolving demand while the two cost components are demand-independent within the same single scalar objective, the effect is partial: the demand's time-variation changes the benefit portion of the objective but not its cost portion.

Access times α and helicopter arrival times T are summed into z2 (eq 2); travel, setup, delay-penalty and repair costs into z1 (eq 1); the priority/repair-time ratio (γ/μ)·λ into z3 (eq 3). Section 4 rewrites each uncertain constraint into its robust counterpart; Section 5 aggregates z1,z2,z3 into one membership-weighted objective (TH) solved in GAMS/CPLEX. Thus decisions → feasibility+timing constraints → objective values, all inside the solver.

*How it realizes the objective.* The assembled model coordinates relief distribution and short-term road repair under uncertainty by making road-repair scheduling and relief routing endogenous to a single optimization: prioritized crew repair (z3) reopens the specific damaged nodes that lie on truck delivery routes; access-time constraints (61-62) force trucks to wait for those repairs (repair time τ added into access time α); and the cost/time objectives (z1,z2) trade off DC establishment, split air delivery to high-risk areas, delay penalties, and crew deployment. Flexibility (temporary DCs re-decided each period; delivery windows up to a due date with penalty) and robustness (interval-polyhedral protection of demand and durations) are built into the same program, so the TH compromise solution is a full multi-day plan that speeds access to high-risk areas while controlling cost and time.

**Q2 — Environment component? Partial.**

There is no standalone simulator or RL environment and, critically, no congestion/traffic-assignment loop. But the constraint block functions as a genuine decision→system-state evaluator: it takes actions (which damaged nodes to repair and in what sequence/by which crew = link reopenings; helicopter routes; truck routes; DC openings; delivery quantities) and returns the resulting realized state — repair completion times τ (constraints 31-32), when each link becomes passable (constraint 60), truck access times α and helicopter arrival times T (constraints 61-62, 16-17), and delivered vs unmet/backlogged demand (qe/el, qg/sl). These outputs form objective z2. It is 'partial' because (a) the mapping is embedded in the MILP constraints and solved jointly with the decisions rather than being an explicit forward-evaluation environment, and (b) all travel times are distance/constant-free-flow-speed, independent of link volume — an evaluation block, not a flow-dependent network-loading model.

- **Input (variable level):** Which links are open/passable: parameter θ_iji' (=1 if damaged node i' lies on route i-j) combined with repair decision λ^t_i summed over prior periods, so a route is usable only if all damaged nodes on it are already repaired (constraint 60). Repair/crew actions: crew-node assignment pe^t_il and crew sequence f^t_ij yielding completion times τ. Routing decisions: helicopter arcs v^kt_ij, truck arcs x^dt_ij. Physical constants: inter-node distances dis_ij; constant free-flow speeds by mode (helicopter η_k, land truck η'_d, crew ω_l, all km/h); service time φ_i; repair durations μ_δil. Demand/capacity: emergency demand de^t_i, consumable demand dg^t_i, helicopter capacity hQ_k, truck capacity tQ_d, DC/supplier capacities, due dates dk^t_i.
- **Output (variable level):** Repair completion time τ^t_jlδ at each damaged node (travel dis/ω + repair μ, chained along the crew tour; constraints 31-32); the time each link becomes passable (gating passage; constraint 60). Helicopter arrival time T^t_jk to each high-risk area (constraints 16-17). Truck access time α^t_jd to each disaster area, which explicitly adds the repair time of any damaged node on the used route plus service and travel time (constraints 61-62) — realized delivery time including waiting for road reopening. Served vs unserved demand: emergency delivered qe and unmet el (constraints 12-13); consumable delivered qg and backlogged sl carried to later periods (constraints 54-58). Aggregate performance: total cost z1, total operation/completion time z2 (= Σα + ΣT, eq 2), total repair-priority z3. There is no link flow/volume or average-speed output; the only 'volumes' are relief-good quantities on vehicles (zd load), not traffic volume.

**Q3 — Traffic metrics & OD demand.** Uses traffic time/speed/volume: **Partial**.

SPEED enters only as constant, mode/crew-specific free-flow speeds (η_k air, η'_d land, ω_l crew, km/h) used to convert distance to travel time — no congestion- or flow-dependent speed. VOLUME/flow: no traffic flow or congestion is modeled; the only 'volumes' are relief-good quantities carried/delivered (zd, qg, qe) and vehicle-arc binaries, not link traffic volumes. So the model uses travel-TIME metrics (with fixed speeds) but not flow/congestion-based traffic metrics.
- *Depends on OD-demand magnitude:* **Yes**
- *How demand magnitude is determined:* Demand is a fixed, author-supplied input matrix, not estimated/gravity/uniform. **Q4 — Does the demand's dynamicness affect the objective function? Partial.**

The model has three objectives. z1 (min total cost) contains a backlog-penalty term π·sl_i^t on unmet consumable demand and a penalty term π'·el_i^t on unmet emergency demand. sl_i^t and el_i^t are quantities (number of items) driven by the per-period demand magnitudes dg_i^t and de_i^t through constraints (12)-(13) (el_i^t = el_i^{t-1} + de_i^t − Σ_k qe) and (55)-(56) (sl_i^{t+1} ≥ Σ_{t'≤t} dg_i^{t'} − Σ delivered). Because demand is time-varying, a higher demand in a period yields more backlogged/unmet items and thus a larger penalty, so the demand's dynamicness scales z1's value. In contrast, z2 (min total completion/access time: Σ α_jd^t + Σ T_jk^t) and z3 (max repair prioritization Σ (γ_i^t/μ_δil)·λ_i^t) are computed on time and priority/repair-time quantities that are NOT weighted by the demand magnitude, so their values do not change with the demand's time-variation. Hence one objective term depends on the dynamic demand while the others do not → partial.

*Connections.* Sequential, layered pipeline solved as one monolithic MILP. (1) A hurricane scenario feeds HAZUS-MH, producing per-component damage states and expected/worst/best GIS damage maps (user may adjust). (2) The civil single-period max-flow disruption model uses those damage states plus network topology to compute which components function, met vs. unmet demand, and interdependency binaries y that propagate outages across systems (Constraints 26-30). (3) The restoration scheduling layer (Constraints 14-25) chooses repair binaries beta_ijt/beta_jt and crew assignments alpha over T periods - this is the decision that reopens arcs/nodes over time. (4) Those beta values drive the three social sub-models: for public safety and commerce, beta sets which transportation arcs/intersections are passable and at what damage-gated time cost, then a min-cost multicommodity flow returns met demand + travel-time cost; for community services, beta and neighbor relationships set node performance directly (no flow). *How it realizes the objective.* Maximize the weighted aggregate performance of the three social-infrastructure categories over a discrete recovery horizon - maximize sum_{s in SN} WS*Pi_s + sum_{s in SD} WS*Pi_s + sum_{s in SG} WS*Pi_s (Eq. 4 = Eq. 31) - by optimally choosing WHICH damaged civil components to repair, in WHAT time-period schedule (beta), and with WHICH crew assignment (alpha), subject to network-flow, scheduling, physical-interdependency, damage, and supply/demand constraints. For public safety Pi_s rewards met emergency-service demand (heavy weight HW on the unmet-demand penalty) and penalizes total travel-time cost; for commerce Pi_s is met customer-demand flow minus delivery cost; for community services Pi_s is weighted node performance from damage. Stakeholder weights WS let a decision maker prioritize a system (e.g. EMS=1, others=0).

**Q2 — Environment component? Yes.**

There is a genuine decision->system-state mapping, embedded inside the MILP objective/constraints (the classic 'hidden' network-loading of an optimization paper). Given a repair decision that fixes which transportation arcs/intersections are passable in each period (repair binaries beta_ijt for arc (i,j), beta_jt for intersection j) and their damage-gated travel-cost tier cijz, the public-safety block computes a min-cost multicommodity flow of emergency services from stations to demand nodes and returns performance = heavy-weighted unmet-demand penalty plus total travel-time cost (Eq. 60); the commercial block returns met customer-demand flow minus delivery cost (Eq. 84). This is decision -> network passability/cost -> flow / served-demand / time -> performance. Important nuances: (a) it is one monolithic MILP that solves repair scheduling AND the flows SIMULTANEOUSLY, so the environment is not a separately-callable loader/simulator; (b) it is a single-period min-cost flow per time step, NOT an iterative user-equilibrium / BPR assignment, and congestion is not flow-dependent; (c) the community-services sub-model has NO flow environment - node performance is set directly from damage state and neighbor relationships.

- **Input (variable level):** Repair schedule as component-availability binaries: beta_ijt (arc (i,j) repaired/passable by period t) and beta_jt (intersection j repaired) - a fully damaged arc blocks flow on both z=1 and z=2 until beta=1; a low-damaged arc blocks only the z=1 (low-cost) tier, forcing flow onto z=2 (Constraints 38-41). Also: damage-gated arc time-cost tier cijz (z=1 low cost but usable only when undamaged, z=2 high cost); combined arc/node capacities um_ij, um_i; station supply sS_i / adjusted SS_it; adjusted per-node demand DS_it (bound on met demand); node priority weights wS_i. For commerce: depot supply, distribution-center capacity/carryover remainder omega, delivery frequency vartheta and delivery cost DC.
- **Output (variable level):** Public safety: the multicommodity service flows xS_ijzt on each arc; met demand vS_it at each demand node (bounded by adjusted demand DS_it); total travel-TIME cost sum cijz*xS_ijzt; and the heavy-weighted unmet-demand penalty HW*sum wS_i*(vS_it-DS_it) - together giving system performance Pi_s. Commerce: met customer demand = flow xS2_ijt out of demand nodes minus delivery costs, giving Pi_s. Aggregated and normalized, these become per-system performance percentages recovering over the horizon (paper reports post-event lows of 72% EMS, 80% police, 66% fire, 0% banking, 10% fuel, 56% healthcare, 52% education, rising as repairs proceed; banking dips at t=2 because road damage around the primary banking center prevented ATM replenishment).

**Q3 — Traffic metrics & OD demand.** Uses traffic time/speed/volume: **Yes**.

- *Traffic metrics used:* The public-safety environment computes traffic TIME and FLOW on the road network: cijz is the time cost of traversing arc (i,j,z) and the objective explicitly sums 'the amount of time cost that the emergency services uses in responding to the demand'; xS_ijzt are service-flow (volume) variables bounded by arc capacities um_ij. IMPORTANT correction to the draft: congestion is NOT flow-dependent and there is no volume-delay/BPR function and no speed variable. The index z in {1,2} is a fixed per-arc cost tier (z=1 lower cost, z=2 higher cost) whose USABILITY is gated by DAMAGE state, not by traffic volume - a low-damaged arc is forced onto the higher-cost z=2 tier, a fully damaged arc is unusable on both tiers until repaired. So the 'congestion' cost is effectively a damage-induced travel-cost penalty. The other computed traffic-relevant quantities are served vs. adjusted demand (vS_it vs DS_it) and, for commerce, delivered customer-demand flow xS2. Community services use no traffic metric (node performance from damage).
- *Depends on OD-demand magnitude:* **Yes**
- *How demand magnitude is determined:* Demand is a per-node PARAMETER, not a gravity-estimated or zonal OD trip matrix. Each demand node i in a social system carries a fixed baseline 'normal demand level' dS_i (integer), sized from the CLARC County dataset (realistic design guidelines, population distribution, critical-facility siting/response-time criteria). The flow is a supply-node(station Vs+)-to-demand-node(Vs-) min-cost multicommodity transportation/transshipment problem: supply bounded by SS_it at stations, met demand bounded by adjusted DS_it at demand nodes (Constraints 32-35). Travel-time and unmet-demand terms depend directly on this nodal demand magnitude (met demand vS_it <= DS_it; penalty proportional to DS_it - vS_it). - *Spatial/temporal resolution:* Spatial: per demand node / per facility within each social system (station-to-demand-node level), each node carrying its own demand dS_i / DS_it and priority weight wS_i - NOT per-OD-pair and not a zonal OD matrix. Temporal: discrete time periods t=1..T, each a 6-hour interval, with T=20 giving a 5-day recovery horizon (results reported over the first ~15 periods).
- *Other demand notes:* Demand is aggregate/integer at each node (no individual agents). Public-safety unmet demand carries a heavy weight HW so responders always answer calls regardless of travel-time cost. A distinctive feature is that social-infrastructure demand can INCREASE after the event (contrasted in Table 1 with civil systems whose 'demand tends to be consistent and predictable' and whose 'damage ... has little or no effect on demand'), and demand can be generated across systems (commercial/utility state -> new public-safety demand). The flow is directional (stations -> demand nodes) but is a nodal supply/demand transportation problem, so there is no origin-destination trip matrix; 'OD dependence' here means the time/served-demand metrics depend on the adjusted nodal demand magnitudes.

**Q4 — Does the demand's dynamicness affect the objective function? Yes.**

The paper maximizes a weighted sum (Eq. 4) of three social-infrastructure performance functions, each of which is a sum over time periods t=1..T. For public safety (Eq. 1) the per-period term is an unmet-demand penalty HW × w_i × (v_it − D_it) minus a travel-time cost, where D_it is the "adjusted demand level for service s at node i in time period t" and v_it is the amount met that period. Because the objective explicitly sums the shortfall (v_it − D_it) with D_it indexed by t, any time-variation of the demand magnitude directly changes the objective value: in a period where D_it rises (e.g., interdependency-generated demand such as a gas-station line or powered-down intersection creating new police demand), the achievable met-demand term and the incurred penalty both change. Similarly the commercial-services objective (Eq. 2) sums per-period met demand w_i × x^{s2}_ijt (the flow of customer demand out of the demand nodes) minus delivery cost, and the community-services objective (Eq. 3) sums per-period node performance w_i × π_it. Thus the dynamic (time-varying) demand is not confined to constraints/state; it is the very quantity being accumulated in the objective, so its dynamicness propagates into and scales the objective value. This is the subtle "yes" case: a demand-weighted served/unmet-demand integral summed over periods where demand changes each period.
